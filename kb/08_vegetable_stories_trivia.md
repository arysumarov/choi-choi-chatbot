# Vegetable Stories and Fun Trivia (十大蔬菜圖鑑)

Source: the team's "Illustrated Guide and Knowledge Base of 10 Vegetables" (2026). Fun facts and history stories for Choi Choi to tell. When a story is a legend, say "it's said…" / 「據講…」.

When someone asks about one of these vegetables: tell ONE short story or fun fact first, then a useful fact (season, how to pick it, a cooking idea from file 09), then invite them to find it at the farmers' stalls.

---

## 1. 菜心 Choy Sum
- **A flowering relative of bok choy (白菜).** Choy sum is the tender flowering stalk that shoots up from the centre of the plant when it gets ready to bloom.
- **Why "菜心" (vegetable heart)?** It comes from the most tender, central part of the plant – in Cantonese, 「心」 means the very best part.
- **Winter choy sum is sweeter.** In cold weather the plant turns some of its starch into sugar to protect itself from the cold, so winter 遲菜心 has a natural sweet aftertaste.
- **History:** native to southern China (Guangdong, Guangxi) and grown for over 1,000 years. In the Qing dynasty, choy sum from Guangzhou's Honam (河南, today's Haizhu) and Zengcheng (增城) was famous across Guangdong, Hong Kong and Macau. In Hong Kong today, blanched choy sum with a spoon of cooked-oil soy sauce (熟油豉油) is a must at cha chaan tengs and family dinners.

## 2. 芥蘭 Kai-lan / Gai Lan (Chinese broccoli)
- **Broccoli's close cousin.** Kai-lan and broccoli are the same species (*Brassica oleracea*) – that's why both have crunchy, juicy stems.
- **The slight bitterness** comes from natural compounds (glucosinolates). Cantonese people say kai-lan 「開胃」 – it wakes up your appetite.
- **How to pick it:** stems with only 1–2 little white flowers open are the most tender. If the whole cluster has bloomed, the stem is old and tough.
- **Name story (it's said):** 「芥藍」 is named for its thick, blue-green leaves – a natural white waxy coating makes them shimmer blue-green in the sun. Its famously crunchy stems inspired the classic Cantonese dish 炒芥蘭度 (stir-fried kai-lan stems, sliced diagonally).

## 3. 西洋菜 Watercress
- **Not originally Chinese!** It comes from Europe and Western Asia; because it arrived from 「西洋」 (the West), it was simply called 西洋菜 – "Western vegetable".
- **It grows in clean, flowing shallow water.** The little white roots on its stems are "aerial roots" for taking in nutrients from water – not mould, and a sign it's fresh.
- **Nutrient-density champion:** in a 2014 US CDC study ranking 41 "powerhouse" fruits and vegetables by nutrient density, watercress was No. 1 with a perfect score of 100, ahead of Chinese cabbage, chard and spinach.
- **History (it's said):** watercress came to Macau and Hong Kong in the mid-19th century. One story says a Cantonese man named Wong Cheong (黃昌) tasted it in Portugal and brought seeds back to Zhongshan and the New Territories (e.g. Fanling, Yuen Long). It loved the local climate and became Hong Kong's favourite autumn–winter soup vegetable – 西洋菜陳腎湯 (watercress soup with dried duck gizzard).

## 4. 番茄 Tomato
- **Fruit or vegetable?** In 1893 the US Supreme Court decided (in a tax case) that although a tomato is botanically a fruit, in everyday cooking and in law it counts as a "vegetable".
- **Cooking helps:** cooking tomatoes with a little oil breaks the cell walls, so your body absorbs much more of the red pigment lycopene (茄紅素) than from raw tomato. Raw tomato has more vitamin C.
- **How to pick it:** a green, curly stem cap means fresh; a star-shaped dent at the bottom often means thicker, juicier flesh.
- **History:** tomatoes come from the Andes of South America; the Aztecs called them "tomatl". When Spanish explorers brought them to Europe in the 16th century, many Europeans were scared to eat them because they're related to poisonous nightshade plants, so they grew them as decorations. Their old scientific name *Lycopersicon* means "wolf peach". It took until about the 18th century for tomatoes to become popular food in Europe.

## 5. 南瓜 Pumpkin
- **The part we eat** is the thick fruit wall that protects the seeds. Roasted pumpkin seeds make a crunchy snack with zinc and magnesium.
- **Why is Japanese kabocha (栗子南瓜) so fluffy?** It has more starch and less water than ordinary pumpkins, so when steamed it tastes like chestnut.
- **How to pick a sweet, mature pumpkin:** press your fingernail into the skin. Rock-hard with a white powdery bloom = ripe and sweet. If it dents easily, it's a young pumpkin – better for stir-frying.
- **History:** one of the oldest farmed crops, from Central and South America – ancient squash seeds found in Mexico are thousands of years old. It came to China around the Ming dynasty; because it came "from the south", it was called 南瓜. Fun fact: the Irish first carved Halloween lanterns from turnips, and switched to pumpkins after moving to North America, where pumpkins were big and easy to carve.

## 6. 苦瓜 Bitter Melon (涼瓜)
- **The "gentleman's vegetable" (君子菜):** cook it with pork ribs, beef or eggs and it keeps all its bitterness to itself – it doesn't make the other food bitter.
- **The most bitter part is the white spongy pith inside**, not the green flesh. Scrape it out with a spoon before cooking and it's much less bitter.
- **How to pick it:** big, plump bumps = thicker flesh and milder taste; small, sharp, dense bumps = more bitter.
- **History and names:** bitter melon comes from tropical Asia and has been grown in southern China for centuries. In Cantonese, 「苦」 sounds unlucky (hardship), so people renamed it 涼瓜 ("cooling melon"). Hong Kongers also call it 半生瓜 ("half-life melon"): you dislike the bitterness when you're young, and only learn to love its bittersweet taste after half a life of experience.

## 7. 白蘿蔔 Daikon / White Radish
- **One radish, three tastes:** the top near the leaves is juicy and sweet (good raw or in salads); the middle is sweet and crisp (good for soups and steaming); the pointed tip is the most peppery (good for pickling or stir-fried strips).
- **Cut off the leaves as soon as you get home!** Otherwise the leaves keep pulling water out of the root and the radish goes hollow and dry inside (「糠心」).
- **How to pick it:** of two radishes the same size, choose the heavier one – it's juicier and not hollow.
- **History:** an ancient vegetable grown in China for thousands of years, mentioned in the *Book of Songs* (詩經). In Hokkien and Chiu Chow it's called 「菜頭」, which sounds like 「好彩頭」 (good luck) – that's why turnip cake (蘿蔔糕) is a must at Lunar New Year. And on cold Hong Kong winter streets, radish soaked in chu hou sauce (柱侯蘿蔔) at beef-offal stalls is a classic comfort food.

## 8. 秋葵 Okra
- **The sticky "slime"** inside okra is mostly soluble fibre (pectin) and mucilage – it's normal, don't be scared of it.
- **Cut it across and you get a little five-pointed star** – a favourite cute topping for bento boxes.
- **Cooking tip:** blanch okra whole, without cutting it or removing the stem cap, then slice it after cooking. It stays crisp and keeps its juices inside.
- **History:** okra comes from north-east Africa (around Ethiopia) and was grown in ancient Egypt. It travelled along trade routes to the Mediterranean, India and the Americas. The famous US Southern stew "gumbo" gets its name from a West African (Bantu) word for okra, *ki ngombo*.

## 9. 菠菜 Spinach
- **The Popeye story:** for decades people believed spinach was super high in iron because a scientist put a decimal point in the wrong place (35 mg instead of 3.5 mg), which supposedly inspired Popeye. Fun twist: historians later found that this "decimal point" story is itself mostly a myth! Spinach has a normal amount of iron.
- **What spinach really has a lot of:** folate, vitamin K and lutein (a pigment found in the eye).
- **That chalky feeling on your teeth** comes from oxalic acid (草酸). Blanching spinach in boiling water for about 10 seconds before stir-frying removes a lot of it.
- **History:** spinach comes from ancient Persia (today's Iran). In 647 AD the King of Nepal sent it to the Tang dynasty court as a gift; it was called 「波斯草」 ("Persian herb"), later shortened to 菠菜. It's said the Qianlong Emperor gave it a poetic nickname – 「紅嘴綠鸚哥」 ("red-beaked green parrot") – because of its red roots and green leaves.

## 10. 紫洋蔥 Red Onion
- **Why onions make you cry:** it's the onion's self-defence! Cutting its cells mixes enzymes with sulphur compounds and releases a gas; in your eyes it forms a mild irritant, so your brain makes tears to wash it away.
- **No-tears trick:** chill the onion in the fridge for about 20 minutes before cutting (it slows the enzymes), or cut it near running water.
- **Red vs yellow onion:** red onions have extra purple pigments (anthocyanins) and a milder, slightly sweet taste – great raw in salads.
- **History:** onions have been grown for over 5,000 years. In ancient Egypt their rings-inside-rings were a symbol of eternity. Workers who built the pyramids were given onions and bread, and archaeologists found small onions placed in the eye sockets of Pharaoh Ramesses IV's mummy.
