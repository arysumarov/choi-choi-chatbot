# Fieldwork: What Farmers and Customers Told Us

Source: the team's interviews at the Central Farmers' Market on 21 September 2026 (2 farms, 1 farming couple, 2 customers). Customers are kept anonymous. These are people's own words and experiences, not official KFBG information.

How Choi Choi should use this file:
- Use the farmers' stories when people ask about vegetables, seasons, prices, weather or "meet the farmers". Say "a farmer told my friends…" or "Andrew from Pig Kun Farm says…".
- Customers' opinions are opinions. Never repeat health claims from interviews (e.g. "more nutritious", "healthier"), and never use them to put down other food.

## Andrew – Pig Kun Farm 豬君農園 (Tai Tong 大棠, Yuen Long)
(Our transcript wrote the name as 聚觀農場; it is Pig Kun Farm, the youngest and smallest farm on KFBG's list, HKORC certified.)

- **Before farming, Andrew was a head chef at a big Chinese restaurant (大酒樓).** As a chef he always looked for fresh, safe ingredients, and that led him to organic vegetables.
- **Being a farmer was his dream since he was a child.** Funny part: as a kid he really did NOT like eating vegetables! He grew a few plants in pots at home and joined the gardening club in secondary school, but still didn't eat what he grew. Only as a grown-up did he start loving vegetables.
- At first he farmed with organic methods without a certificate; later he got **HKORC (香港有機資源中心) certification** to make his dream "even better".
- The farm is **run by his own family, on rented land**. They don't hire workers. Because they are short of hands, **the farm is not usually open to visitors**.
- **Why organic costs more (Andrew's own explanation):**
  1. Organic fertiliser costs much more than chemical fertiliser.
  2. Chemical fertiliser makes plants grow very fast. With organic fertiliser, "a plant needs as many days as it needs", so it grows more slowly and yields are much lower.
  3. Insect losses: the farm is a natural ecosystem with lots of bugs. He sometimes uses organic pesticides made from plant extracts or natural bacteria, but they don't affect most kinds of insects, so a lot of the crop gets eaten.
  4. "We sell at a higher price, but we don't actually earn more money." (laughs)
- **Weather is his biggest challenge:** black rainstorms three times in a row (黑雨三連擊), extreme heat, and two No. 10 typhoons. Watermelons and other melons drown easily in floods and can't be saved. Climbing crops and beans survive flooding but stop growing for about a month before they recover.
- **Second challenge: selling.** Growing it is one thing; selling it is another. He says the Central Farmers' Market is "a really good sales platform". It's enough to break even; to earn more he also joins pop-up markets, e.g. at Elements (圓方), Tai Po Mega Mall, MOKO (新世紀廣場), Wan Chai, Hong Kong Organic Day (全港有機日), the FarmFest (漁農美食嘉年華) and a watermelon festival at Maritime Square (青衣城).
- He told us he also sells on **Wednesdays**, but KFBG's official market day is **Sunday**. Visitors who want to find him on another day should check Pig Kun Farm's own social media first.
- He uses Facebook, Instagram and Threads, and notices each one reaches different people: Facebook → older people, Instagram → mums, Threads → young people. Customers of all ages buy from him.
- **His message to students and young people:** "Hong Kong can grow high-quality, unique and safe farm produce." He also says the farm's promotion "can definitely be better" and he'd welcome new ideas from young people.

## Rose – Blue Girl Organic Farm (Yuen Long)
(Our transcript wrote the name as 藍莓有機農場.)

- **The farm was started by Rose's mum, Flora.** Flora felt vegetables in shops didn't taste like the vegetables she ate as a child in her home village, and she wanted good food for the family's children. So the family started growing their own vegetables, grew more and more, learned by doing, and finally became an organic vegetable farm. It has been organic for over ten years.
- Rose says organic farming is close to the "original" way of farming: **no chemical fertiliser, no pesticides, no weedkiller, and no other sprays**, and they choose **local seed varieties**.
- **Best seller: sweet potato leaves (番薯葉)** – the farm's No. 1 vegetable for a long time, especially in summer. In spring and summer they also sell sweet potato seedlings; in autumn and winter, spinach seedlings (菠菜苗) and kale (羽衣甘藍).
- **Where else to buy:** pick-your-own and direct sales at the farm, a stall in Hung Hom market (紅磡街市; not open every day, mostly afternoons – check their Instagram for times), YATA supermarket (一田), and a sales point at YOHO Mall in Yuen Long.
- **Why organic costs more (Rose's explanation):** expensive land rent in Hong Kong (the farm rents its land), high wages, hiring foreign workers because few locals want to farm (minimum wage plus travel and application fees), more spending on fertiliser, and **weeding and catching bugs by hand** – they often catch bugs **at night**. Small fields are hard to work with big machines, so they rent machines only when needed and do most jobs by hand. She feels the price is fair for what you get.
- **Weather:** this year (2026) there were three months of extreme heat and heavy rain – it rained all of July, it was over 30°C every day for three months, and at least 35°C on the farm. Some crops were burnt by the sun, some "cooked" by the heat, some drowned. The farm had **almost no harvest for about three months**, but still had to pay rent and wages.
- Customers: mostly people over 40 who care about local, organic food. Younger visitors more often buy fruit or simple ready-to-eat food. Some new customers found the market through Instagram.

## A farming couple from Kam Tin 錦田, Yuen Long
(They had been selling at the market for about half a year. The farm's name was not recorded.)

- **What they grow follows the seasons:**
  - Late summer / early autumn: winter melon (冬瓜), 菜仔 (small leafy greens), water spinach (通菜), amaranth (莧菜). In September the melons were nearly finished.
  - Winter: choi sum (菜心), gai lan (芥蘭), cabbage (椰菜), tomatoes (番茄), broccoli (西蘭花), cauliflower (椰菜花). "These only grow once winter comes – you can't grow them in summer."
- **How they attract customers:** "We just sell honestly (老實賣嘢)." People try organic vegetables, find they taste good, and come back.
- **Happiest moment:** when customers come back and say "that was delicious!" and buy again. They already have regular customers.
- **Honest about worms:** sometimes a melon looks perfect but a bug has eaten the inside. If a customer tells them, they make it up to them. "Organic produce can't be fully controlled."
- **Hard parts:** two or three heavy rains can wipe out all the melons; this year their cherry tomatoes rotted in the rain; extreme heat makes growing harder. And Hong Kong has very few places where local organic vegetables can be sold.
- **What they want people to know:** "Farmers work really hard. It's not simple." They want more people to know about Hong Kong's local farmers.

## What customers told us (anonymous)

- **Who comes:** many regulars, mostly 40–50+, but people around 30 come too. Many come on purpose because they know organic vegetables are sold here.
- **A weekly regular** (coming for about three years): supermarket imported veg is much cheaper, but she comes to support local organic farming and farmers who have worked for 10–20 years and want to keep going. She would recommend the market to friends.
- **A regular of more than 10 years** trusts the market because it's organised by KFBG and the farmers "follow the rules", sell honestly and don't overcharge. Favourites: **bitter melon (苦瓜), bok choy (白菜) and choy sum (菜心)** – "they have the best bok choy and choy sum". The **organic eggs are very popular**: there's a queue and a limit per person. Buy with the seasons: "In winter there are lots of vegetables; in summer it's mostly melons – you drink a lot of winter melon soup." She also spreads her shopping across several farmers "so everybody gets a little business".
- **What customers wish could be better:** longer opening hours (it starts at 11:00), more farmers' stalls rather than chain shops, and easier transport for farmers – farms are far away and bringing produce to Central is hard.

## Seasonal guide (from the farmers we met)
- **Summer (about Jun–Sep):** winter melon and other melons/gourds, water spinach 通菜, amaranth 莧菜, sweet potato leaves 番薯葉, small leafy greens 菜仔. Melon soup season!
- **Autumn–winter (about Oct–Mar):** choi sum 菜心, gai lan 芥蘭, cabbage 椰菜, bok choy 白菜, spinach 菠菜, kale 羽衣甘藍, broccoli 西蘭花, cauliflower 椰菜花, tomatoes 番茄. Farmers and customers agree winter has the most vegetables.
- What's on the tables on any day depends on the weather – heavy rain or heat can wipe out a crop – so the farmers at the stall know best.
